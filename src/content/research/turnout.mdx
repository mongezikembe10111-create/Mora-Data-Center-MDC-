<h2 id="different-questions">Different questions, different populations</h2>

“Turnout” sounds like a single measure. In practice, its meaning depends on the population used in the calculation. Votes or participating voters divided by registered voters describe participation among the people on an election’s voters’ roll. Participating voters divided by eligible citizens describe participation across a wider potential electorate. <sup><a href="#reference-1" aria-label="Source reference 1">[1]</a></sup>

Those measures can both be useful. They answer different questions. A report should identify the numerator, denominator, election, geographic coverage and observation date every time it presents a rate. The certified voters’ roll can differ from the effective election turnout denominator when the IEC reports additional voters, including MEC7 additions. Use the source-defined population rather than silently substituting the certified snapshot.

**Analytical interpretation:** a change in turnout does not, by itself, reveal whether the change came from more people voting, a different number of registered voters, or both. Inspect the counts alongside the percentage.

<h2 id="follow-the-denominator">Follow the denominator</h2>

Consider an **illustrative population of 100 eligible citizens**, of whom 70 are registered and 35 vote. The turnout rate among registered voters is 50%. Participation among eligible citizens is 35%. A registration gap is eligible citizens minus registered voters. A participation gap is eligible citizens minus participating voters. Registered non-participation is the compatible election voter population minus participating voters. Where additions change the turnout denominator, that election population must be distinguished from the certified-roll snapshot. These quantities require compatible dates and definitions. A recent voters’ roll total cannot simply be subtracted from a population estimate for a different year and labelled a current gap.

<h2 id="ballots-and-voters">Ballots are not people</h2>

Election systems may give a voter more than one ballot. Adding ballot totals across different contests can therefore count one person more than once. A party’s valid-vote total is also not a count of every voter who attended: rejected or spoiled ballots can make the totals differ.

Where a report uses a particular ballot as a turnout proxy, retain that definition. Do not silently turn it into an independently verified count of unique participants. For local elections, check which municipal or ward ballot and voting arrangement each source covers.

**Reporting standard:** The Public Square preserves source-defined ballot measures and published turnout, labels derived calculations and leaves unavailable stages of the participation funnel empty. For example, a municipal report’s combined ballot-paper total is not enough to fill a missing count of participating people. “Official comparable data unavailable” is more informative than a plausible invented count.

<h2 id="comparison-checklist">A comparison checklist</h2>

Before comparing two rates, check five things:

1. Do the numerators count the same event: people voting, ballots cast, or valid votes?
2. Do the denominators cover the same legal and demographic population?
3. Are the elections, observation dates and boundary vintages compatible?
4. Does a published percentage include rounding or a source-specific turnout definition?
5. Is the change expressed as a percentage change or a percentage-point change?

A move from 50% to 45% is a decline of **5 percentage points**, and a **10% relative decline** in the rate. These are hypothetical examples of arithmetic, not historical turnout values.

The aim is not to choose the most striking number. It is to choose the measure that answers the question and retain enough context for another reader to reproduce it.
